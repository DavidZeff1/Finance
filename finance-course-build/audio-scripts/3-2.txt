Growth rates look boring. One and a half percent versus three percent barely moves a chart over a year or two. But you already know from chapter one what happens when a rate compounds. The gap goes from rounding error to canyon. Long run growth is just the future value formula applied to an entire country.

So let's run the numbers. Two countries both start at ten thousand dollars of GDP per person. Country A grows at one and a half percent a year, country B at three percent. After sixty years, one working lifetime, A reaches about twenty four thousand four hundred dollars and B reaches about fifty eight thousand nine hundred. Same starting point, and B's citizens end up two point four times richer, from a difference you would barely notice in any single year.

The rule of seventy says it faster. Divide seventy by the growth rate to get the doubling time. At one and a half percent, living standards double every forty eight years. At three percent, every twenty four. And look what that does to real countries. China grew at roughly eight percent per person from nineteen eighty to twenty twenty, so income per head multiplied about twenty one times. Japan, from nineteen ninety one, grew at about zero point eight percent, so forty years bought a forty percent gain. Neither country was badly run. The difference is a few percentage points a year, held for four decades.

Or take nineteen sixty, when South Korea and Ghana had roughly similar income per person. Both poor, both recently out of colonization or war. Korea then compounded at some of the fastest rates ever recorded for three decades. Today Korean income per person is roughly seven times Ghana's. No single year did that. The exponent did.

So what sets the growth rate? In the long run the answer is almost embarrassingly simple. Productivity. Output per hour worked. A country gets richer per person only if each hour of work produces more stuff. And productivity grows through better machines, better recipes, better trained people, and institutions that make investing worthwhile. Courts, property rights, competition.

Which is why economists get so worked up about policies with supposedly small effects. Over forty years, two percent versus two point three percent growth is a twelve and a half percent gap in income per person. A permanent zero point three point boost to productivity, from broadband, or containerization, or plausibly from A I, is a serious national question, not a rounding error.
