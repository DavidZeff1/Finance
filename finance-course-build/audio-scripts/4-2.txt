Here's the fact that surprises almost everyone. Most money is created not by the government printing it, but by commercial banks making loans. When a bank approves your nine hundred dollar loan, it does not hand you someone else's savings from a vault. It simply credits your account with nine hundred dollars. A keystroke. Your deposit is brand new money that did not exist a moment earlier. Loans create deposits, not the other way around.

Let me show you the bookkeeping, because it's the clearest way to see it. A bank approves your two hundred thousand dollar mortgage. On its balance sheet, loans outstanding rise by two hundred thousand, and customer deposits rise by two hundred thousand. That's it. The bank typed two numbers. It created an asset, your promise to pay, and a liability, the deposit in your account, in the same keystroke. No existing depositor's balance went down. The money supply just grew by two hundred thousand dollars out of nothing but a contract.

The textbook then chains this into a loop called fractional reserve banking. If banks hold ten percent of each deposit as reserves, then a thousand dollar deposit supports nine hundred of lending, which gets deposited, which supports eight hundred and ten, and so on. It's a geometric series, and the limit is the initial deposit divided by the reserve ratio. One thousand over zero point one, so ten thousand dollars of deposits.

But treat that multiplier as a teaching device, not a description of modern banking. The United States cut reserve requirements to zero in March twenty twenty. Today banks are limited by capital rules, risk appetite, and whether profitable borrowers actually exist. Causality mostly runs backwards. Banks find creditworthy borrowers first, create the deposits, and obtain reserves afterwards.

Money creation also has a dark twin. Money destruction. Because a bank promises deposits back on demand but has locked the funds into long term loans, a maturity mismatch, no bank can survive everyone asking at once. If depositors merely believe a bank might fail, they rush to withdraw, forcing it to dump assets at fire sale prices, which causes the failure they feared. That self fulfilling stampede is a bank run.

Two defenses exist. Deposit insurance, up to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars per depositor, removes the reason for small depositors to run at all. And the central bank stands as lender of last resort for solvent banks caught short. Neither is foolproof. In March twenty twenty three, Silicon Valley Bank's clients, mostly startups with balances far above the insured cap, tried to pull forty two billion dollars in a single day, coordinating on social media and moving money by app.
