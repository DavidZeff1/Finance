Can a government go broke? It depends entirely on what currency the debt is in.

A government that borrows in its own currency, the United States, Japan, the United Kingdom, can always print the money to pay. A technical default on own currency debt is a choice, or a political accident like a debt ceiling standoff, never an arithmetic necessity. A government that borrows in someone else's currency can genuinely run out. Argentina's dollar debts have defaulted repeatedly. Greece in twenty ten discovered that the euro is effectively a foreign currency for its members, because Athens cannot print euros, and its private creditors eventually took a haircut of more than fifty percent.

But can always print is not the same as can safely print. Covering deficits with newly created money, monetization, taxes everyone who holds the currency through inflation. Economists call that revenue stream seigniorage. Inflation is the escape valve and it does work, because it quietly shrinks the real value of old fixed rate debt. But push it hard and you get scrambled price signals, savaged savers, and eventually lenders who demand double digit yields or refuse your currency altogether.

Look at the four outcomes side by side, because the dividing line is sharp. Japan carries debt above two hundred and fifty percent of GDP, the highest in the developed world, and its ten year yields spent a decade near zero. No default, no crisis. It borrows in money it issues. Greece, in the euro, saw yields above thirty percent and a restructuring that wrote down around two hundred billion euros, the largest sovereign default in history, with GDP falling about twenty five percent. Argentina, borrowing dollars, has defaulted nine times since independence, because dollar debt cannot be inflated away. It must be earned or refinanced. Sri Lanka in twenty twenty two ran out of foreign exchange reserves and defaulted, and a currency problem became a physical one. Fuel and medicine shortages.

So the dividing line is not how much debt, but whose money it is promised in. The moment a government owes something it cannot create, its solvency depends on earning foreign exchange, and that is a hard budget constraint identical to a company's. The literature calls this original sin, and it explains most emerging market crises of the last fifty years.

One final caution though. Own currency status buys patience, not immunity. In September twenty twenty two the United Kingdom announced large unfunded tax cuts. Within days, thirty year government bond yields jumped more than a full percentage point, the pound slumped, and pension funds hit margin spirals severe enough that the Bank of England had to intervene. The prime minister was gone within weeks. No default, no arithmetic crisis. Just the bond market repricing fiscal credibility in real time.
