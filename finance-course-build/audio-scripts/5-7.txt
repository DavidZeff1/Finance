An exchange rate is just a price. Yen per dollar. Set in the largest market on Earth, with over seven trillion dollars changing hands every day.

Under a floating exchange rate, the dollar, euro, yen, pound, that price moves continuously with supply and demand. Under a currency peg, the Hong Kong dollar or the Gulf currencies, the central bank promises a fixed rate and stands ready to buy or sell reserves to defend it. Pegs deliver stability until they don't. A peg abandoned under speculative attack, Britain in nineteen ninety two, Thailand in ninety seven, snaps in a violent devaluation.

What moves a floating currency? Four forces dominate. Interest rate differentials: if American rates rise above Japan's, holding dollars pays better, capital flows in, the dollar strengthens. Inflation differentials: a country inflating at thirty percent a year watches its currency lose purchasing power and eventually exchange value. Trade and capital flows: persistent demand for a country's exports or assets bids up its currency. And risk appetite: in a panic, money stampedes into dollars, yen and Swiss francs, the safe haven currencies, regardless of fundamentals.

The long run anchor is purchasing power parity. Identical goods should cost the same everywhere once converted, or arbitrage would fix it. The Economist's Big Mac index turns this into a joke that works, because the burger is a standardized basket of tradable ingredients, local labour and rent. If a Big Mac costs five dollars sixty nine in America and four hundred and fifty yen in Japan, the implied rate is seventy nine yen per dollar. When the market rate is a hundred and forty eight, the yen looks about forty seven percent undervalued. Parity deviations persist for years, because haircuts and rent don't ship, but the index reliably flags extremes.

Now let me show you why the yen was sitting there, because this is where it gets dangerous. The carry trade. Borrow a hundred million yen at zero point one percent. Convert at a hundred and forty eight, giving about six hundred and seventy five thousand dollars. Buy American Treasury bills at five point three percent. A year later you've earned about seven hundred and eleven thousand dollars, you pay back the yen, and you pocket roughly five point two percent with no market view at all.

As long as the exchange rate holds. If the yen strengthens to a hundred and forty, your dollars buy back fewer yen and the entire profit is gone. At a hundred and thirty two you lose about six percent. And leverage of five to ten times is normal in this trade, so a five percent currency move becomes a twenty five to fifty percent loss.

This is not hypothetical. In early August twenty twenty four, an unexpected Bank of Japan hike plus weak American data strengthened the yen sharply in days. Carry positions unwound at once, and Japan's Nikkei fell twelve point four percent in a single session, its worst day since nineteen eighty seven. Carry trades earn a small premium most of the time and lose enormously, all at once, occasionally. Picking up nickels in front of a steamroller. That shape recurs across finance.
