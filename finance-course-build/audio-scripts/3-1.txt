Alright, let's zoom out. In the last chapter you studied one market at a time. One good, one supply curve, one demand curve. Macroeconomics adds everything up, and it asks three questions: how much are we producing, what do things cost, and who has a job? This section is about the first one, and the master number is GDP, gross domestic product. The market value of every final good and service a country produces inside its borders in a year.

Two words there are doing all the work. Final means we count the loaf of bread but not the flour that went into it. Otherwise we'd count the wheat, then the flour, then the bread, and triple count the same value. And inside a country means geography, not ownership. A German owned BMW plant in South Carolina counts in American GDP.

So how do you measure it? Add up who spends the money. GDP equals C plus I plus G plus N X. Consumption, investment, government purchases, net exports. For the United States, call it twenty nine trillion dollars, the proportions are remarkably stable. Consumption is about sixty eight percent, so the American economy is mostly households buying things. Investment is around eighteen percent, and be careful here. Investment means new factories, equipment, software and housing. It does not mean buying stocks. In GDP language, trading an existing asset creates nothing new. Government purchases are about seventeen percent, roads and teachers and defense, but not social security cheques, because those are transfers, not purchases. And net exports run about minus three percent.

Now here's the trap that follows you through all of macro. Prices change. If GDP rises six percent but everything got six percent more expensive, nothing real happened. Nominal GDP uses today's prices. Real GDP strips inflation out. So if nominal GDP grows six percent and the deflator says prices rose four percent, real growth is about one point nine percent. A headline that looked like a boom was a sluggish year plus inflation.

One last thing. GDP is a speedometer, not a health checkup. It tells you how fast the machine is running, and nothing about who is in the car. A parent's childcare counts as zero. The same care bought from a daycare counts fully. Wikipedia adds enormous value and roughly nothing to GDP. Useful gauge. Incomplete one.
