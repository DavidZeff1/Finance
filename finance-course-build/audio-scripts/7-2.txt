The headline size of a public company is its market capitalization. Share price times shares outstanding. A fifty dollar stock with four billion shares is a two hundred billion dollar company. A five hundred dollar stock with twenty million shares is a ten billion dollar one.

Which kills the first myth. The share price alone tells you nothing about how big or how expensive a company is. Price per share is just market cap divided by an arbitrary share count. The same pizza cut into eight slices or eighty.

Analysts bucket companies as large cap, above ten billion, mid cap, two to ten, and small cap, below two billion. Size matters because it correlates with risk, liquidity, and how many analysts bother covering the name.

Now, not all shares are equal. Companies can issue multiple share classes with different voting rights. Alphabet's Class A shares carry one vote each, Class C carry none, and the founders' Class B shares carry ten. That lets insiders keep control while selling most of the economics. At Meta, Mark Zuckerberg holds roughly thirteen percent of the economics and about sixty one percent of the votes. An unremovable majority. Snap went further: its twenty seventeen listing was the first ever of entirely non voting stock. So when you buy a share, check what you're buying. The ticker on the screen is often the class with the least power, and activist investors will fix this company is simply false when the founder controls a voting majority.

Your ownership percentage isn't fixed either. When a company issues new shares, to raise cash, to pay employees in stock, or to fund an acquisition, every existing share becomes a smaller fraction of the company. That's dilution.

And here's why it's the quietest way a shareholder loses money: the share price never needs to fall for it to happen. Take a company with a hundred million shares and a hundred million dollars of net income. Earnings per share is exactly one dollar. Now issue three million shares to staff each year and buy back two. After five years the count is a hundred and five million, and earnings per share is ninety five cents. Profits never fell, yet earnings per share dropped four point eight percent. Your slice shrank from one percent to zero point nine five two without you selling anything.

Which is why analysts watch the net change in diluted share count, not the buyback headline. A company announcing two billion of repurchases while issuing two point two billion of stock compensation is not returning capital. It is running a payroll through the equity account. You can check this in thirty seconds on any filing: take diluted weighted average shares from the income statement this year and five years ago. Flat or falling while revenue grows means discipline. Climbing three to five percent a year means you are funding compensation.
