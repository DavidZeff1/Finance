Here's the part textbooks skip. In markets, macro data is not trivia. It is a scheduled stream of events that reprices trillions of dollars within seconds of release. And the key mechanic is this: prices already reflect the consensus estimate before the number drops. So markets do not move on whether the data is good. They move on whether it beats or misses expectations. A bad jobs number that is less bad than forecast can spark a rally.

Let me give you the calendar worth memorizing. C P I, monthly at eight thirty a m Eastern, sets expectations for central bank rate moves. The jobs report, first Friday of the month, is the single most watched release, and nonfarm payrolls is its headline number. The P C E price index is the Federal Reserve's own preferred inflation gauge, less famous than C P I and more important to policymakers. GDP arrives quarterly and is backward looking, so it's usually less explosive. The F O M C rate decision, eight times a year, is the policy response itself. And weekly jobless claims every Thursday are the most timely labour signal you have, though noisy enough that you should watch the four week average.

Two analyst habits to steal. First, always ask, versus what? A payrolls print of plus one hundred and eighty thousand means nothing until you know consensus was plus two hundred thousand, which is a miss, or plus one hundred and fifty thousand, which is a beat. Second, know the regime. In a good news is bad news market, a strong jobs report can sink stocks, because it implies rates stay high for longer. Same number, opposite reaction, depending on whether investors fear recession or inflation more.

Watch a C P I release play out. Consensus is plus zero point two percent for the month. If the print lands exactly there, everything is roughly unchanged, because that was already in the price. If it comes in hot at plus zero point four, two year yields jump fifteen to twenty five basis points, the S and P falls one to two percent, and the dollar strengthens. Cool print, and all three run the other way. Inflation was high in all three cases. Only the surprise mattered.

And if you build models on this data, respect two engineering realities. Releases get revised. Payrolls are routinely restated by tens of thousands of jobs over the following two months, so backtesting on today's cleaned up series quietly leaks the future. Serious quant shops use point in time, or vintage, datasets that store what was known on each date. And engineer your features from surprises, actual minus consensus, never from levels. The level was already priced.
