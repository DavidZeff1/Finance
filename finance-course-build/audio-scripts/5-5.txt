Now switch from one government's books to the links between countries. The deepest result in trade economics is comparative advantage. Both sides gain from trade even when one of them is better at producing everything. What matters is not who is best, which is absolute advantage, but each country's opportunity cost.

Take two countries, Acadia and Bolandia, each with a hundred and twenty labour hours, making chips and shirts. Acadia needs two hours per chip and three per shirt. Bolandia needs eight and four. So Acadia is faster at both. But every chip costs Acadia only two thirds of a shirt, while it costs Bolandia two whole shirts. So Acadia should make chips, and Bolandia, whose shirts cost it only half a chip, should make shirts.

Run the numbers. Splitting hours evenly with no trade, the world produces thirty seven and a half chips and thirty five shirts. Reassign the same two hundred and forty hours, Acadia mostly chips and Bolandia all shirts, and output rises to forty five chips and forty shirts. Nothing new was invented. The schedule just improved. Trade ten chips for twelve shirts and both countries end up consuming more of both goods. This is a positive sum assignment problem, not a tug of war.

So why is trade politically radioactive? Because the gains are diffuse and the losses are concentrated. Every consumer saves a little on cheaper shirts. The shirt town loses its factory, and its workers cannot costlessly become chip designers. The China shock research found American regions exposed to Chinese import competition after two thousand suffered deep, persistent hits to employment and wages. Trade grows the pie and simultaneously creates identifiable losers. Both statements are true, and honest policy debates are about compensation and transition, not about denying either half.

Which brings us to tariffs, a tax on imports. The crucial question is incidence. Who really pays? The exporter writes no cheque to customs. The importer does. And the twenty eighteen to nineteen American tariffs are the best measured experiment in modern history, because economists watched import prices at the dock in near real time.

The findings were remarkably consistent. Pass through was essentially complete. Prices of tariffed goods at the border rose by close to the full tariff, so the tax was paid by American importers and, downstream, American buyers. The bill was roughly fifty billion dollars a year, about four hundred dollars per household. And the washing machine case is the cleanest. Washer prices rose about twelve percent, and dryer prices rose by a similar amount, though dryers were never tariffed, because retailers price them as a pair. Roughly eighteen hundred manufacturing jobs were created at an estimated consumer cost of around eight hundred and fifteen thousand dollars per job.

None of that says tariffs are never justified. It says the cost is concrete, measurable, and lands mostly at home.
