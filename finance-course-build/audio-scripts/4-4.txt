The fed funds rate itself applies only to overnight loans between banks. Its power comes from transmission. The chain reaction that carries it into every corner of the economy. Let me walk the channels.

Consumer borrowing. Banks price loans off their own funding cost. The prime rate, the benchmark for credit cards and many business loans, sits mechanically at fed funds plus three points, so every hike passes through within days. Thirty year mortgages price off long term Treasury yields rather than the overnight rate, but expectations drag them along. When the Fed turned hawkish in twenty twenty two, mortgage rates went from below three percent to about seven percent in a year.

Business investment. Companies greenlight projects when expected returns beat the cost of capital. Raise rates and the hurdle rises, so marginal factories, data centres and startups don't get funded. Asset prices. Stocks and bonds are priced by discounting future cash flows, so a higher discount rate mechanically lowers today's value, hitting long duration growth stocks hardest. That's one reason twenty twenty two was brutal for tech. The dollar. Higher American yields attract global capital, bidding up the dollar, which makes imports cheaper and exports pricier. And expectations. If firms and workers believe the Fed will hold inflation at two percent, they set prices and wages accordingly. Belief does half the work.

Now the catch that makes central banking genuinely hard. The lag. Milton Friedman's phrase is that monetary policy works with long and variable lags, and most estimates say twelve to eighteen months for a hike to fully hit inflation and jobs. The Fed is steering by looking through a delayed feed. Today's inflation print reflects decisions from over a year ago. Tighten until inflation visibly breaks and you have almost certainly overdone it.

Think of it as a thermostat wired to a radiator with a fifteen minute delay. Crank the heat until the room finally feels warm and you have guaranteed a sauna. Good central banking, like good control engineering, means acting on the forecast, not the current reading, and accepting that you'll sometimes be wrong in public, at scale.

Let me price the transmission so it stops being abstract. Take a household that can afford twenty five hundred dollars a month on a thirty year fixed mortgage. In January twenty twenty one, at two point six five percent, that bought a six hundred and twenty thousand dollar loan. By late twenty twenty three, at seven percent, the same payment bought three hundred and seventy six thousand. Their income never changed. Purchasing power fell about thirty seven percent in two years.

And that creates the lock in effect. A family holding a three percent mortgage cannot move without repricing to seven, so they don't list the house. Supply collapses, prices stay high despite brutal affordability, and the hike that was supposed to cool housing partly freezes it instead.
