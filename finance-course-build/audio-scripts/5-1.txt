Fiscal policy is the demand side lever the last chapter didn't cover. Where the central bank changes the price of borrowing, the government changes spending and taxes directly. Build a bridge, cut payroll taxes, mail out cheques. The money lands in specific hands immediately, with no waiting for banks to pass a rate cut along.

Some of it happens with no vote at all. Automatic stabilizers are built in negative feedback. In a recession, incomes fall, so tax bills fall, while unemployment insurance and food assistance payments rise. The deficit widens on its own and props up spending. In a boom the same machinery runs in reverse. Discretionary stimulus, the CARES Act of twenty twenty, the two thousand nine Recovery Act, requires Congress to act, which means recognition lags, debate lags, and implementation lags.

Think of it this way. Automatic stabilizers are autoscaling. Traffic spikes, capacity adjusts, nobody gets paged. Discretionary stimulus is paging a human to provision servers by hand. Potentially much bigger, but slow, and sometimes the extra capacity arrives after the traffic spike has passed.

So how much bang per dollar? The fiscal multiplier captures the knock on effects. Pay a contractor a hundred billion to build roads. The contractor's workers spend part of their new income at restaurants, the restaurant owners spend part of theirs, and so on. If each recipient spends sixty cents of every marginal dollar, the naive total is a geometric series. A hundred divided by one minus zero point six, so two hundred and fifty billion. You know this series. It's the same recursion as expected visits in a Markov chain.

Real world estimates land far below two and a half, and they disagree, for structural reasons rather than sloppy econometrics. Money leaks out each round into savings, taxes and imports. If the economy is already at full capacity, extra demand mostly bids up prices instead of output. And if the central bank fears inflation, it raises rates to offset the stimulus, deliberately shrinking the multiplier toward zero.

Empirical estimates cluster between zero point five and one point five, and the composition matters enormously. Unemployment benefits run around one to nearly two, because they go to people who spend nearly all of it immediately. Tax cuts to high earners and corporations run around zero point two to zero point six, because much of it is saved or buys existing assets.

Two conditions swamp the policy choice. Slack, and the central bank's reaction. In two thousand nine, with unemployment near ten percent, extra spending pulled idle workers and factories into use. In twenty twenty one, with unemployment falling fast and supply chains jammed, the same dollars met a wall, so more of the impulse came out as prices. So when two economists quote different multipliers, ask what state of the world each one assumed.
