In December two thousand eight the Fed hit a wall. The fed funds rate reached zero and the economy was still collapsing. You can't meaningfully cut below zero, because savers would rather hold cash. That constraint is called the zero lower bound. So the Fed reached for a second lever. Instead of changing the price of overnight money, change the supply and price of long term money directly.

That lever is quantitative easing. The central bank buys huge quantities of long term government bonds and mortgage backed securities, paying with newly created reserves. Extra demand pushes bond prices up, and, as you'll master in the fixed income chapter, when a bond's price rises its yield falls. Lower long term yields mean cheaper mortgages and corporate debt even when the overnight rate is already at zero. Investors who sold their safe bonds to the Fed then reach for other assets, easing conditions further. And the purchase programme itself is a loud signal that rates will stay low, which shapes expectations.

The reverse gear is quantitative tightening. The Fed lets bonds mature without buying replacements, so reserves quietly drain out of the system month by month. No dramatic sales. More like slowly releasing pressure from a valve, precisely because everyone remembers how violently markets react to abrupt moves.

The scale is worth staring at. Before two thousand eight the Fed held under one trillion dollars in assets. Three rounds of easing took it to about four and a half trillion by twenty fourteen. Then the pandemic. In twenty twenty the Fed bought a hundred and twenty billion dollars of bonds per month at the peak, and the balance sheet touched nearly nine trillion in twenty twenty two.

Now, a pitfall worth inoculating against. People say quantitative easing is just printing money, so hyperinflation is coming. But it creates bank reserves, not wallets full of cash, and reserves sit inside the banking system. They don't buy groceries. A decade of massive easing, two thousand nine to twenty nineteen, coincided with inflation stubbornly below two percent. The twenty twenty one burst had easing in the mix, but alongside supply chain chaos and trillions in direct fiscal transfers. Cheques that, unlike reserves, really did land in spendable accounts. Be suspicious of one variable explanations of inflation.

And there's a market structure lesson here that matters for your models. Japan ran this experiment to its logical end. The Bank of Japan now owns roughly half of all outstanding Japanese government bonds and became the largest owner of Japanese equities. When the largest buyer is a price insensitive institution with an unlimited balance sheet, the yield stops being a clean signal about growth and inflation. Any model that reads a quantitative easing era yield as pure market expectation is reading a partly administered price.
