Every security lives two lives. It is born in the primary market, where the issuer sells it and receives the cash. It then spends decades in the secondary market, changing hands between investors while the issuer gets nothing further. When Apple stock trades ten billion dollars in a day, Apple's bank account does not move a cent.

The flagship primary market event is the I P O. A private company hires investment banks to run underwriting. The banks value the company, write the prospectus, a legal disclosure document you can read free at sec dot gov, drum up demand from institutions in a roadshow, set the price, and buy the shares from the company to resell to investors. Bond issuance works the same way but faster and far more routine. Large firms tap the bond market the way you'd call an A P I, sometimes raising billions in an afternoon.

Let me do the economics. A company sells ten million new shares at twenty dollars, so gross proceeds are two hundred million. The underwriters' fee, called the gross spread, is typically seven percent, so fourteen million, and the company nets one hundred and eighty six million. Now if the stock closes its first day at twenty six dollars, that thirty percent pop means the company arguably left ten million times six, sixty million dollars, on the table. Money captured by the investors who got allocations instead of by the firm. Underpricing is one of the best documented puzzles in finance.

Three real cases show the whole range. Airbnb in December twenty twenty priced at sixty eight dollars and closed its first day near a hundred and forty five, up about a hundred and thirteen percent. Had it priced where the stock actually traded, it would have raised roughly twice as much. Facebook in twenty twelve is the opposite extreme. Priced at thirty eight with enormous retail demand, Nasdaq's systems failed for hours, and the stock fell below eighteen within four months. And Spotify in twenty eighteen skipped the ritual entirely with a direct listing. No new shares, no underwriters. It raised zero, because it did not need money, it needed liquidity for existing holders.

Notice what all three have in common. The I P O price is a negotiated price, not a market one. Only from the second day onward is the market talking. Which is why the I P O popped a hundred percent is a statement about the bankers' pricing, not about the company.

And why does the secondary market matter so much if issuers get nothing from it? Because liquidity there is what makes the primary market possible. You will only buy a thirty year bond at issue if you know you can sell it on Tuesday. Secondary prices also do the economy's homework, continuously revaluing every firm, which tells capital where to flow next. That's the price signal machinery from microeconomics, operating at millisecond resolution.
