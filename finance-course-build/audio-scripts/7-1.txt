A share of stock is a fractional ownership claim on a corporation. If a company has issued one billion shares and you own a hundred of them, you own a ten millionth of every factory, every patent, every dollar of profit it ever earns. Equity is the formal word for that ownership interest.

Two legal features make this claim interesting. First, shareholders are the residual claimants. Employees, suppliers, tax authorities and lenders all get paid first, and shareholders get whatever is left. That's why equity is riskier than debt, and why it can pay so much more. Second, limited liability caps your downside at what you paid. If the company collapses owing billions, your shares go to zero but nobody comes for your house. Unlimited upside, floored downside. That asymmetry is the deal.

Think of a company's claims as a priority queue. Debt holders enqueued first get paid first, with a fixed payout. Shareholders sit at the back holding a claim on whatever remains. And that's why the value of a share is so sensitive to small changes in expectations. If a firm is worth a hundred and owes ninety, equity is worth ten. If the firm's value rises just ten percent, to a hundred and ten, equity doubles to twenty.

Now, residual claimant is not a metaphor. The capital stack is an abstraction right up until a company fails, at which point it becomes a payout table. Lehman Brothers, two thousand eight: bondholders got roughly twenty to thirty five cents on the dollar after years of litigation. Shareholders got zero. General Motors, two thousand nine: unsecured bondholders received equity in the new GM, and old GM shares were cancelled. Zero. Enron: partial recovery for bondholders, zero for shareholders, including employees whose retirement accounts held company stock.

There is one famous exception. Hertz filed for bankruptcy in twenty twenty, retail investors piled into the supposedly worthless stock, and then used car prices exploded. The fleet turned out to be worth far more than anyone assumed, so there was money left after every creditor was paid, and equity holders got about eight dollars a share. That's the exception that proves the rule. In the overwhelming majority of bankruptcies there is nothing left, which is exactly what last in line means.

So why does anyone accept that deal? Because of the flip side. A bondholder's best possible outcome is being repaid in full. A shareholder's upside is unbounded. Limited liability caps your loss at what you invested, and the residual claim leaves your gain uncapped. Every equity investment you will ever make is that single trade.

And it's why companies sell pieces of themselves in the first place. Equity is money you never have to repay. A founder sells thirty percent to investors and keeps seventy percent of something big instead of a hundred percent of nothing. Borrowing, by contrast, creates a fixed obligation that can bankrupt you in a bad year.
