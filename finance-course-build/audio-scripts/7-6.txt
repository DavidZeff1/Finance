Companies return cash to shareholders in two ways. Dividends you've already met. A buyback, or share repurchase, is the mirror image of dilution. The company buys its own shares on the market and retires them, so every remaining share owns a bigger slice.

Let me run both side by side, because it's the cleanest way to see they're nearly the same thing. A company earns a hundred million dollars, has a hundred million shares, so earnings per share is one dollar, trading at twenty dollars. It decides to return fifty million.

As a dividend, that's fifty cents per share in cash. Share count stays at a hundred million, earnings per share stays at a dollar, and you hold your hundred shares plus fifty dollars of cash, on which you owe tax this year. As a buyback, the company buys two and a half million shares at twenty dollars and cancels them. Share count falls to ninety seven and a half million, earnings per share rises to one dollar two point six cents, and you still hold a hundred shares, now a bigger slice, with tax deferred until you sell.

Economically these are near identical. The differences are tax timing and optionality. The buyback lets each shareholder choose whether to take cash by selling, or increase ownership by doing nothing. Which is why buybacks overtook dividends as the dominant American payout method, helped by the fact that cutting a dividend is a scandal while pausing a buyback is a footnote.

But there's a catch, and it's a real one. A buyback creates value only if the shares are bought below intrinsic value. Buy back at twenty what is worth thirty and continuing holders gain. Buy back at thirty what is worth twenty and you have transferred wealth from the people who stayed to the people who sold. Airlines spent roughly forty five billion dollars on repurchases in the decade to twenty nineteen and then requested government support in twenty twenty. Apple, by contrast, has retired well over a third of its shares since twenty twelve, and anyone who simply held has watched their ownership percentage rise every single year.

Then there's the stock split, which changes nothing real at all. A four for one split turns one five hundred dollar share into four shares of a hundred and twenty five. Your stake, the market cap, and the P E are all identical. Splits exist mainly for optics and round lot convenience. Which buries the myth for good. A five hundred dollar stock is not more expensive than a fifty dollar one, and a split does not make a company cheaper, although markets sometimes pop on split announcements anyway.

And how do companies get onto the market? The classic route is the I P O, where banks value the company and sell new shares, collecting fees of four to seven percent. A direct listing skips the underwriting machine entirely. And a S P A C is a listed shell full of cash hunting for a private company to merge with, taking it public through the back door. S P A Cs boomed in twenty twenty and twenty one with lighter disclosure, and the average post merger investor lost badly. A useful reminder that innovations in market plumbing often redistribute money toward whoever designed the plumbing.
