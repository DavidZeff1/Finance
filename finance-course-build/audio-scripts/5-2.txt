These two words get mangled daily, and the distinction is one you already know from programming. The budget deficit is a flow. The shortfall this year, like the delta in one iteration of a loop. The national debt is a stock. The accumulator variable. The deficit is the derivative, the debt is the integral. Which means a government can shrink its deficit every single year while the debt still grows, exactly as a function can rise while its slope falls.

Raw dollar figures are nearly useless. Thirty six trillion dollars of debt tells you nothing without a denominator, just as five hundred milliseconds of latency means nothing without knowing the workload. The right scale is the debt to GDP ratio. Debt divided by the annual income of the whole economy. A household owing three hundred and sixty thousand dollars is drowning on a forty thousand dollar income and comfortable on nine hundred thousand. American federal debt sits around a hundred and twenty percent of GDP. Japan carries roughly two hundred and forty. Germany about sixty three.

Now, who is actually owed the money? Mostly not foreign rivals. Mostly the country's own institutions. About twenty percent is held by American government accounts, like the Social Security trust fund, so one arm of government owes another. About twelve percent sits at the Federal Reserve from quantitative easing, and that interest largely flows back to the Treasury. Around forty three percent is American private investors. Funds, banks, pensions, insurers, households. Only about a quarter is foreign. So we owe it to ourselves is roughly three quarters true. That doesn't make debt free, because interest still redistributes from taxpayers to bondholders, but it changes what kind of problem it is.

Let me put real numbers on the budget, because abstractions dissolve the moment you see line items. Recent American federal outlays, roughly six point seven five trillion dollars. Social Security is about twenty two percent. Medicare thirteen. Defense thirteen. Net interest on the debt, also thirteen. Medicaid nine. Everything else, thirty. Revenue was about four point nine trillion, so the deficit was one point eight trillion, roughly six point four percent of GDP.

Three things jump out. One, net interest passed defense spending for the first time in decades. That's the interest rate arithmetic of the next subsection arriving as a budget line. Two, Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid and interest are roughly fifty seven percent of outlays and are not voted on annually, so the annual appropriations fight covers a minority of the budget. Three, a six point four percent deficit while unemployment was near four percent is historically unusual. Deficits that size normally appear in recessions.
