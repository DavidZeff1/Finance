Practitioners split the industry into two camps, and that split shows up in every single job posting you will ever read.

The buy side manages money. Asset managers, hedge funds, pension funds, private equity, venture capital, insurers, sovereign funds. Its product is investment performance, and it earns fees on the assets it runs. The sell side sells services to the money. Investment banks, brokers, market makers, research desks. Its products are execution, new securities, advice and analysis, and it earns fees, commissions and spreads.

The economics differ sharply, and this is the useful part. Buy side revenue scales with assets under management. A fund charging half a percent on fifty billion dollars earns two hundred and fifty million a year whether markets are open or not. Sell side revenue scales with activity. Deals closed, trades executed, spreads captured. Which is exactly why sell side hiring booms with deal cycles while big asset managers grind on steadily.

Let me price four actual seats, because job postings make far more sense once you can see the revenue paying for them. An index fund manager running five hundred billion at a four basis point fee earns two hundred million with maybe three hundred staff. That's zero point seven million per head. An active equity manager on ten billion at half a percent earns fifty million with forty staff, so one point three million per head. A hedge fund with two billion up fifteen percent earns around a hundred million with twenty five staff. Four million per head. And a mergers team closing one billion dollar deal at a one point two percent fee earns twelve million with six people over six months.

Four consequences fall straight out of that table. Index managers must be ruthlessly automated, because zero point seven million per head cannot support armies of analysts, which is precisely why they hire engineers rather than stock pickers. Active managers live and die on assets, so their worst year is one of outflows, not one of losses. Hedge funds have violently variable revenue, which is why compensation is bonus heavy and teams are small. And bankers earn nothing until a deal closes, which explains both the hours and the brutal cyclicality.

Career wise, the sell side hires analysts into investment banking, into sales and trading, and into research. The buy side hires portfolio managers, research analysts, and, in force since the twenty tens, quantitative researchers and data scientists who build signals from data. Sell side experience is a classic on ramp to buy side seats.

Here's the line worth remembering for your own search. The buy side pays you to be right. The sell side pays you to be useful. Data and quant roles now sit on both, but the highest leverage seats for someone with your background are where revenue per head is high and headcount is small, which is exactly where a reproducible pipeline replaces five people with spreadsheets.
