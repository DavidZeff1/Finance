Now zoom all the way in, to the data structure at the centre of every modern market. The order book. And it is literally two priority queues. On one side sit bids, resting offers to buy, sorted highest first. On the other sit asks, resting offers to sell, sorted lowest first. The gap between the best bid and the best ask is the bid ask spread, and the total size resting at each price level is the book's depth.

You interact with it through two order types. A market order says fill me now at whatever prices are available, so it consumes resting orders starting from the best price. A limit order says fill me only at this price or better, and if nobody is willing, it joins the queue and waits. Matching follows price time priority. Best price wins, and among equal prices, first come first served. It's a stable sort you could implement in an afternoon, running at nanosecond scale.

Let's walk the book. Say there are two hundred shares offered at a hundred dollars and five cents, four hundred at a hundred ten, three hundred at a hundred fifteen, and a hundred at a hundred twenty. You send a market buy for a thousand shares. You lift all four levels, and your size weighted average fill is a hundred dollars and eleven and a half cents. That's six and a half cents per share worse than the best ask, sixty five dollars of cost, and it's called slippage. Your own order moved the market. That's price impact, and it's exactly why large investors slice big orders into thousands of small ones.

Who keeps the queues populated? Market makers. They post bids and asks simultaneously, hoping to buy at ninety nine ninety five and sell at a hundred and five cents all day, earning the spread as compensation for the risk that prices lurch while they're holding inventory. Most are now high frequency firms whose edge is speed. Co located servers, microwave links between cities, market data parsed in hardware. The good news is that spreads on liquid American stocks fell from around twelve cents in the nineteen nineties to about one cent today.

Now here's the number that kills strategies. Price the cost explicitly. A retail investor buying a hundred shares of a hundred and fifty dollar stock with a one cent half spread pays about two dollars round trip on fifteen thousand. One point three basis points. Genuinely negligible. But trade it two hundred times a year and it's two point seven percent. Cost per trade is small. Cost per strategy depends entirely on turnover.

Now a fund buying fifty million dollars of a stock trading two million shares a day. Five hundred thousand shares is twenty five percent of daily volume, so you cannot simply lift the offer. Realistic total cost: two basis points of half spread, thirty five of market impact, one of commission. Thirty eight basis points of implementation shortfall. Which means a strategy that backtests at plus fifty basis points per trade is a plus twelve strategy in production, and a losing one after fees. That gap between paper and live returns is where most quant strategies die, and modelling it is a well paid specialty called transaction cost analysis.
