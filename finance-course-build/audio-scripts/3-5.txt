Zoom out on any real GDP chart and you see a rising trend with a wobble around it. That wobble is the business cycle. An expansion climbs to a peak, activity contracts through a recession down to a trough, and then recovery begins the next expansion.

Here is the most important thing to understand about it. The cycle is recurring, but it is not periodic. Post war American expansions have lasted anywhere from one year to over a decade, averaging about five years, and recessions average roughly ten months. So anyone selling you a fixed length cycle clock is selling astrology.

Who decides when a recession officially happened? In the United States it is the N B E R, a committee of economists who date the peaks and troughs using a dashboard of monthly data. Payrolls, real income, industrial production, real spending. Not the folk rule about two negative GDP quarters. And here's the catch. They only rule when the evidence is unambiguous, which means after the fact. The twenty twenty recession ended in April of that year, and they did not declare it until July of twenty twenty one. Fifteen months later. By the time a recession is official, markets moved on long ago.

Which is exactly why analysts obsess over indicator timing. Leading indicators, things like building permits, new factory orders, jobless claims and the yield curve, tend to turn months before the economy does. Coincident indicators like payrolls tell you where you are right now. And lagging indicators, like the unemployment rate and C P I inflation, only confirm what already happened. Unemployment often keeps rising well after a recovery begins, because firms rehire cautiously.

The most famous leading indicator deserves its own moment. The yield curve plots government bond interest rates by maturity, and normally long rates sit above short rates. When the curve inverts, when short rates rise above long rates, bond investors are collectively betting the central bank will be cutting in future, which usually means they expect a downturn. An inversion has preceded every American recession of the past six decades, typically by six to twenty four months.

But now the analyst's lesson, and it's a lesson about data, not economics. A lead time anywhere between six and twenty four months makes this useless as a trading trigger. A fund that sold equities on the two thousand five signal missed two full years of gains. And a signal with roughly eight independent observations cannot be backtested to any useful confidence, no matter how clean the chart looks. The twenty twenty two inversion, the longest on record, is a live reminder that the mechanism itself can change.
