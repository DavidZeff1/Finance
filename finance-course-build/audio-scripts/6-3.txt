Back in the microeconomics chapter you met market failures. Information asymmetry, externalities, monopoly power. Financial regulation is the institutional response to exactly those failures. Securities rules force disclosure, which fixes information asymmetry. Bank capital rules limit systemic risk, which is a negative externality, because a bank's collapse hurts people who never banked there. And market structure rules police concentrated venues.

In America each regulator owns one slice. The S E C watches securities markets. Stocks, bonds, funds, disclosure. It exists because investors can't verify claims themselves, so issuers must publish audited truth. The Federal Reserve watches banks' safety alongside monetary policy, because bank failures cascade and someone must be lender of last resort. The F D I C insures deposits up to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars per depositor, per bank, which stops runs before they start. FINRA polices brokers and their salespeople. And the C F T C watches derivatives and commodities futures, because leverage there can blow holes in the real economy.

Now notice the pattern, because this is the part that matters. No single regulator sees everything. A bank holding company might answer to the Fed for its balance sheet, the S E C for its brokerage arm, the C F T C for its swaps desk, and the F D I C for its deposits. That fragmentation is a recurring plot device in crisis post mortems, because risky activity migrates to whichever corner is watched least. The name for that is regulatory arbitrage.

Look at the record. Madoff, two thousand eight. The S E C received detailed tip offs from Harry Markopolos from nineteen ninety nine onward and did not act. Disclosure regimes need someone who actually reads the disclosures. A I G, two thousand eight. Its derivatives unit was an insurance company writing bank style risk, supervised by neither a bank regulator nor a securities one, and the rescue cost a hundred and eighty two billion dollars. LIBOR rigging. A rate underpinning hundreds of trillions of contracts was set by a daily survey with no regulator and no transaction data behind it. Archegos, twenty twenty one. A family office was exempt from most registration, so no regulator, and no single bank, could see its total leverage across prime brokers. Nobody acted until ten billion dollars was lost. And F T X, twenty twenty two, combined the roles of exchange, broker, clearing house and custodian. A combination illegal in every regulated market, and unregulated there.

So here's the pattern to internalize. Financial crises rarely happen where regulation is strict. They happen in the seams. Between agencies, between countries, and between the legal categories that a new business model does not fit. Whenever you hear of a fast growing activity that isn't really a bank, go looking for the seam.
